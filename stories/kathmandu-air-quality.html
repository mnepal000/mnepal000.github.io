Choking season: Kathmandu and South Asia's air crisis

Kathmandu keeps topping the world's most-polluted-city rankings. The annual averages tell a quieter story: year after year, the air stays far above anything considered safe. And the youngest pay the highest price. One in five air-pollution deaths in Nepal is a child under five.

On several mornings in recent years, Kathmandu has woken up as the most polluted major city on earth: in May 2024, in April 2025 when PM2.5 hit 204.5 µg/m³, and again in March 2026. The headlines capture the spikes. The slower story is in the annual averages, which show a city breathing unsafe air as a matter of routine.

In 2024, Kathmandu averaged 45.1 µg/m³, equivalent to an AQI of 128 and nine times the WHO guideline. In 2019 the average was 48 µg/m³, and in 2023 it was 41 µg/m³. The numbers move a little year to year, but they never come close to safe.

The worst months are winter. In January 2019, Kathmandu's monthly average reached 102.7 µg/m³, deep into the "unhealthy" range. An ICIMOD analysis found the city breathed unhealthy air on 75 of 90 days in early 2025. The valley's bowl geography traps pollution under winter inversions, while brick kilns, vehicle exhaust, open waste burning, construction dust, and rubble from the 2015 earthquake keep feeding the haze.

Kathmandu's air in an average year is nine times dirtier than the World Health Organization says is safe.

A regional pattern

Kathmandu is not an outlier; it is part of one of the most polluted regions on the planet. In IQAir's 2023 capital city ranking, New Delhi was the world's most polluted capital for the sixth straight year at 92.7 µg/m³, followed by Dhaka at 80.2. Islamabad and Kathmandu sat close together in the low forties.

The 2024 report tells the same story: New Delhi remained the most polluted capital at 91.8 µg/m³, with Dhaka next at 78. Across Asia, 97.7 percent of cities exceeded the WHO guideline in 2024. South Asia's smog is a shared airshed problem, drifting across the Indo-Gangetic plain regardless of borders. Infectious disease specialist Dr. Anup Subedee put the stakes bluntly in January 2026: "Air pollution is killing at a far higher rate than COVID-19 did during its worst years in Nepal. People are dying in their twenties of cancer, or having heart attacks and strokes in their thirties and forties."

The smallest lungs suffer most

Nepal's Ministry of Health attributes about 42,000 deaths a year to air pollution. In March 2026, ministry spokesperson Dr. Prakash Budhathoki said children under five account for 21 percent of those deaths, roughly 8,800 children every year. Adults over 70 make up another 27 percent, according to an earlier ministry statement. The two ends of life, the lungs still forming and the lungs wearing out, carry nearly half the country's air-pollution toll.

Set against the 2021 census, the imbalance is stark. Children under five are 8.4 percent of Nepal's population but account for 21 percent of air-pollution deaths. Nepalis over 70 are just 4.3 percent of the population yet account for 27 percent of deaths, an implied rate of about 907 deaths per 100,000 people, more than ten times the rate for everyone aged 5 to 69. Children under five die at more than four times that middle rate.

During the April 2025 smog, even the Kanti Children's Hospital, the national referral center for ailing children, reported a spike in respiratory illnesses, skin diseases, and other pollution-linked conditions among its young patients.

Children are, as UNICEF Nepal puts it, "hit hardest by the adverse effects of toxic air." They breathe more rapidly than adults and lack fully developed physical defenses, and exposure can put babies' brain development at risk, with effects that can last a lifetime. A review in the Journal of Nepal Paediatric Society found asthma-like symptoms common among Kathmandu schoolchildren, with current wheeze reported in 5 to 9 percent of children across several school studies.

In March 2021, pollution did something it had never done before in Nepal: it closed the schools. With the air quality index topping 300 and in places 500, the government shut every educational institution for four days, affecting about eight million students, and declared a health emergency advising children and older people to stay indoors. "As far as we are aware, this is the first time schools have been closed because of pollution," education ministry spokesman Deepak Sharma told AFP.

The smoke inside the home

For Nepal's children, the most dangerous air is often indoors. The 2022 Nepal Demographic and Health Survey found only 42 percent of households cook with clean fuels; the rest burn wood, dung, or other biomass, filling kitchens with smoke where small children spend their days. The divide is stark: 53 percent of urban households use clean fuel, but only 19 percent of rural ones do.

Peer-reviewed research using NDHS data estimated that about 40 percent of childhood pneumonia cases in Nepal are attributable to polluting cooking fuel, and about 31 percent to the absence of a separate kitchen room.

UNICEF's regional office notes that more than 70 percent of under-five air-pollution deaths in South Asia come from household air, not outdoor smog, and that over 60 percent of South Asians are exposed to it. The haze over Kathmandu makes the headlines. The smoke in the kitchen does the quieter killing.

The health toll

Nepal's Ministry of Health attributes about 42,000 deaths a year to air pollution, with children under five (21 percent, per the ministry's March 2026 statement) and adults over 70 hit hardest. The World Bank estimates it cuts 3.4 years off the average Nepali's life expectancy. But the toll is not only in the mortality tables. It is in the waiting rooms.

"The Kathmandu valley is not a good place for people who are allergic to dust," says Dr. Dirgha Singh Bom. "In fact the number of patients with respiratory problems has increased by 20%." Dr. Kabir Nath Yogi, head of the respiratory ward at the Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital, puts the cause plainly: "Dust particles suspended in the air from construction and vehicle emissions are the main causes of the increasing cases of respiratory problems." His advice to the people who live along the roadside, where the dust is thickest: "No place in the Kathmandu Valley is free from pollution, but those who live in houses by the roadside should be more cautious about their health." Both doctors were interviewed for a PhMuseum photo essay on the city's air.

Nobody breathes more of it than the traffic police. Officers spend long hours each day directing traffic at the city's busiest intersections, and a 2015 study of their lung function found that the longer they performed traffic duty, the greater the harm to their lungs. Deputy Inspector General of Police Prakash Aryal told Earth Island Journal: "My people have to work under extreme circumstances, every time I ask them to go do their duty, I am in a dilemma. As a manager I have a responsibility to ask them to do their job well, but I feel I am exploiting these people knowing the risk to their health."

The April 2025 smog showed what those numbers feel like. Samikshya Sunuwar, a graduate student, told ANI she had "just recovered from a severe cold and cough, and was completely bedridden for two to three days," with itchy, twitching eyes and dust settling on her face on the walk home. Fellow student Suniva Hada reported "serious headaches," red and itchy eyes, and trouble concentrating in lectures. At the Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital, information officer Kaliprasad Osiyara said outpatient departments built for 250 patients a day were seeing 300: "In the last one week, the flow of patients has increased tremendously; we are struggling to manage the flow of patients." Dr. Niraj Bam, head of pulmonology, critical care and sleep medicine at the hospital, said the emergency ward was full, with about 80 patients in intensive care, on ventilators, or on oxygen, and some patients referred to other hospitals for lack of beds.

That week PM2.5 hit 204.5 µg/m³ and Kathmandu topped the world's most-polluted city rankings. The health ministry advised residents to wear masks and limit outdoor activity on bad days.

Policy responses so far are modest: vehicle emission testing, dust control rules for construction, and public advisories. Enforcement is patchy, monitoring outside the valley is thin, and brick kilns and waste burning continue largely unchecked. Until the sources are tackled, the data suggests Kathmandu's choking seasons will keep returning on schedule.

Method and caveats

Annual PM2.5 figures are IQAir city averages from ground monitoring stations. The 2019 figure comes from IQAir's Kathmandu city page; the 2023 figure from the 2023 World Air Quality Report's capital city ranking; the 2024 figure from IQAir's Kathmandu reporting. The "multiples of the WHO guideline" chart divides each annual average by the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³. The peak-day figure (204.5 µg/m³, April 2025) is a single-day reading reported by ANI, not an average. Station coverage in Nepal is concentrated in the Kathmandu Valley, so these numbers describe the capital, not the country. Real-time "most polluted city" rankings reflect hourly conditions and swing wildly; the annual averages are the better measure of chronic exposure. Doctor and police quotations are from named interviews published by PhMuseum and Earth Island Journal, linked below. April 2025 episode accounts are from ANI's reporting. The World Bank life-expectancy figure and the ICIMOD unhealthy-days analysis are those institutions' own estimates, cited with attribution; death-toll estimates vary by source and methodology, and the Ministry of Health's figure of about 42,000 deaths a year is the one used in this story. The under-five death share (21 percent) comes from the MoHP spokesperson's March 2026 statement as reported by the Himalaya Times and Gothalo; the 70-plus share (27 percent) comes from an earlier MoHP statement reported by ANI in April 2025. The age chart shows estimated deaths per 100,000 people, dividing the implied death counts (shares applied to 42,000, rounded) by the census populations; both are estimates and the rates inherit the uncertainty of the Ministry's death shares. Cooking-fuel figures are from the 2022 Nepal Demographic and Health Survey. The pneumonia figures are population attributable fractions from a 2020 peer-reviewed analysis of NDHS data, meaning the estimated share of cases that would not occur without each risk factor, not a share of observed cases.
