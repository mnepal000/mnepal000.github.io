Choking season: Kathmandu and South Asia's air crisis

Kathmandu keeps topping the world's most-polluted-city rankings. The annual averages tell a quieter story: year after year, the air stays far above anything considered safe.

On several mornings in recent years, Kathmandu has woken up as the most polluted major city on earth: in May 2024, in April 2025 when PM2.5 hit 204.5 µg/m³, and again in March 2026. The headlines capture the spikes. The slower story is in the annual averages, which show a city breathing unsafe air as a matter of routine.

In 2024, Kathmandu averaged 45.1 µg/m³, equivalent to an AQI of 128 and nine times the WHO guideline. In 2019 the average was 48 µg/m³, and in 2023 it was 41 µg/m³. The numbers move a little year to year, but they never come close to safe.

The worst months are winter. In January 2019, Kathmandu's monthly average reached 102.7 µg/m³, deep into the "unhealthy" range. The valley's bowl geography traps pollution under winter inversions, while brick kilns, vehicle exhaust, open waste burning, construction dust, and rubble from the 2015 earthquake keep feeding the haze.

Kathmandu's air in an average year is nine times dirtier than the World Health Organization says is safe.

A regional pattern

Kathmandu is not an outlier; it is part of one of the most polluted regions on the planet. In IQAir's 2023 capital city ranking, New Delhi was the world's most polluted capital for the sixth straight year at 92.7 µg/m³, followed by Dhaka at 80.2. Islamabad and Kathmandu sat close together in the low forties.

The 2024 report tells the same story: New Delhi remained the most polluted capital at 91.8 µg/m³, with Dhaka next at 78. Across Asia, 97.7 percent of cities exceeded the WHO guideline in 2024. South Asia's smog is a shared airshed problem, drifting across the Indo-Gangetic plain regardless of borders.

The health toll

Nepal's Ministry of Health attributes about 42,000 deaths a year to air pollution, with children under five and adults over 70 hit hardest. But the toll is not only in the mortality tables. It is in the waiting rooms.

"The Kathmandu valley is not a good place for people who are allergic to dust," says Dr. Dirgha Singh Bom. "In fact the number of patients with respiratory problems has increased by 20%." Dr. Kabir Nath Yogi, head of the respiratory ward at the Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital, puts the cause plainly: "Dust particles suspended in the air from construction and vehicle emissions are the main causes of the increasing cases of respiratory problems." His advice to the people who live along the roadside, where the dust is thickest: "No place in the Kathmandu Valley is free from pollution, but those who live in houses by the roadside should be more cautious about their health." Both doctors were interviewed for a PhMuseum photo essay on the city's air.

Nobody breathes more of it than the traffic police. Officers spend long hours each day directing traffic at the city's busiest intersections, and a 2015 study of their lung function found that the longer they performed traffic duty, the greater the harm to their lungs. Deputy Inspector General of Police Prakash Aryal told Earth Island Journal: "My people have to work under extreme circumstances, every time I ask them to go do their duty, I am in a dilemma. As a manager I have a responsibility to ask them to do their job well, but I feel I am exploiting these people knowing the risk to their health."

During severe episodes, Kathmandu's major hospitals report spikes in respiratory cases; the Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital saw its outpatient numbers jump during the April 2025 smog, when PM2.5 hit 204.5 µg/m³ and the city topped the world's most-polluted rankings. The health ministry has repeatedly advised residents to wear masks and limit outdoor activity on bad days.

Policy responses so far are modest: vehicle emission testing, dust control rules for construction, and public advisories. Enforcement is patchy, monitoring outside the valley is thin, and brick kilns and waste burning continue largely unchecked. Until the sources are tackled, the data suggests Kathmandu's choking seasons will keep returning on schedule.

Method and caveats

Annual PM2.5 figures are IQAir city averages from ground monitoring stations. The 2019 figure comes from IQAir's Kathmandu city page; the 2023 figure from the 2023 World Air Quality Report's capital city ranking; the 2024 figure from IQAir's Kathmandu reporting. The "multiples of the WHO guideline" chart divides each annual average by the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³. The peak-day figure (204.5 µg/m³, April 2025) is a single-day reading reported by ANI, not an average. Station coverage in Nepal is concentrated in the Kathmandu Valley, so these numbers describe the capital, not the country. Real-time "most polluted city" rankings reflect hourly conditions and swing wildly; the annual averages are the better measure of chronic exposure. Doctor and police quotations are from named interviews published by PhMuseum and Earth Island Journal, linked below.
